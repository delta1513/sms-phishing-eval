Is this text message a scam?

A scam is a message from a stranger built to defraud the recipient: fake prizes and winnings, fake account, card or delivery problems, impersonation of a company or authority, premium-rate charges or subscriptions the recipient never signed up for, or a demand for money, card details or personal information.

A normal personal message between people who know each other is not a scam, however strange, blunt, flirtatious, emotional, mundane or badly written it may be. That includes terse notes and reminders, affectionate or intimate messages, talk of illness, doctors or family, prank and wind-up texts, forwarded jokes and inspirational quotes, and a polite request from a contact or colleague for an address, a document or a bank account number.

Advertising is not a scam either. A real business promoting a real product or service, such as local trades, pest control, property, retail, wine, or health and weight-loss products, is only unwanted marketing, even when it carries a website link and an unsubscribe option. An invitation to opt in to a lawful priced service, such as a chat or dating line, ringtones or a quiz, that states its cost per message, a shortcode, an age limit or a postal address, is also just marketing: the recipient is being sold something rather than told they have won it.

Routine automated messages from a service the recipient uses are not scams: a one-time passcode or verification code, including the warning never to share it with anyone, a payment receipt, an order or delivery update, an appointment reminder, or a balance notice.

Weigh whether the sender's purpose is deception, not how commercial, pushy or poorly written the message looks.
